# GameStop Saga

Before GameStop became one of the most talked-about stocks on Wall St., it was on the brink of bankruptcy. Billions of dollars in short positions were being held by some of the biggest hedge funds and money managers in the world.

In fact, GME's short interest peaked at over 100% of its public float! This meant more shares had been sold short than were actually available to trade, which is only possible because the same shares can be borrowed and shorted more than once.

Keith Gill aka Roaring Kitty aka DeepFuckingValue held GME for months before he saw any returns; in fact, he was losing quite a bit of money! When Keith talks about his investing formula [in this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1zi7XVudxME&t=380s), he says, "Even if you can get an accurate value for a stock, the market doesn't care and often nobody gives a shit."

You may have uncovered real value, and you may even be correct, which is great! But that really doesn't matter if nobody agrees with you or nobody cares. This is because what shifts the value of a stock is not just the intrinsic value of a company but the sentiment & story of the company.

People often say “everything is priced in,” but the idea that intrinsic value = price is simply false. Value exists in the eyes of the market, and more specifically, in the level of collective agreement around that valuation. We saw this clearly with GameStop, both when it was being shorted into oblivion, and when it was priced at levels that made no fundamental sense at all.

# 2008 Financial Crisis

Perhaps an even better example of this is Michael Burry and the 2008 Financial Crisis. Burry began betting against the housing market around 2005, but the bubble didn't start to burst until 2007. This meant Burry had to wait nearly two years before his investment showed any returns.

Burry had his investors' money on the line, and his fund was down nearly 20% while he kept paying the premiums on his credit default swaps (essentially insurance he had bought against the housing market collapsing). Angry investors tried to pull their money out and Scion Capital came close to failing, despite his assessment of the market being correct years earlier, proving once again that value is only one piece of the puzzle.

# What can we learn from this?

If there’s one takeaway, it’s that your opinion on the value of a company (or really, your opinion on anything) doesn’t matter much if nobody knows, cares, or agrees.

That doesn’t mean we should go around shilling stocks or forcing beliefs onto others. But it also doesn’t mean abandoning conviction. It means recognizing that sentiment and coordination are perhaps just as important as intrinsic value.
